Syllogism Board

Enter class, property, relation and existence statements. The board draws them as a graph and as nested circle-and-fan diagrams, and checks each argument against the nine valid categorical moods and the rules of relational inference (transitivity, substitution of identicals).

Statement types
ClassA belongs to the class B (Aristotle's category of substance) — every human is an animal. Type the class as a singular noun without an article; the board adds “a / an”.
Property · state · actionA quality, state or objectless action of A (quality · posture · doing) — a knife is dangerous, a bird flies. Type the verb phrase as it reads after a singular subject (“is dangerous”, “flies”); the board negates it and makes it agree with a plural subject. In the graph it becomes the class of things that do so (a green node).
RelationA relation between two things — comparison (the Sun is bigger than the Earth) · space (Seoul is inside Korea) · part–whole (the mantle is part of the Earth) · time (dinosaurs are earlier than humans) · possession · quantity (an ant has 6 legs) · cause · identity · difference · a custom verb (the Earth orbits the Sun). Type the object with its article when it needs one (“a star”, “the Sun”); “a star” and “star” are the same concept.
ExistenceWhether A exists — the unicorn does not exist
Definition · equivalenceTwo concepts pick out the same things — every mammal is by definition an animal that nurses its young. Inclusion runs both ways (≡), so either can replace the other.
OnlyThe quantifier “only” — only humans write = everything that writes is a human. The graph draws this logical direction (writes → human).
Conditional · disjunctionSwitch a statement's form to “conditional” or “disjunction” and it gets two clause slots. Each clause becomes a box in the graph; a conditional is drawn as an “if…then” arrow, a disjunction as an “or” diamond. Rules checked: modus ponens · modus tollens · hypothetical syllogism · disjunctive syllogism. Affirming the consequent, denying the antecedent and affirming a disjunct are reported as fallacies. When both clauses share the same general subject (if every bird …, then …), the board reads it as holding for each individual of that subject.
Inductive setChoose “inductive set” next to a set's name and the two premise rows give way to one conclusion and an evidence panel. An inductive conclusion gets a credence (probability of being true) instead of true/false — a Bayesian update in which a sample enters through a beta distribution, other evidence through likelihood ratios, and background knowledge as a factor on the prior. Separately, it gets a reasoning quality score on criteria ①–⑧. ⑧ robustness is rated per item: a small study or an anecdote that has not been independently replicated is discounted even before anyone disputes it. Each item can also carry a status (valid · corrected · expression of concern · retracted) and the date it was checked. Evidence retracted for error or fraud stays on screen, struck through, but leaves the calculation; it does not count as counter-evidence and does not change ④ total evidence. Items sharing a source with one retracted for fraud are flagged. If inductive sets with contradictory or contrary conclusions get credences that break the rules of probability, the board reports a “probability mismatch”. If the conclusion is causal (“increases …”, “… because of …”), the board reads each item's study design: evidence obtained without intervention (correlational or observational data, cases, theory or mechanism) can be at most “weak”, and observational data with confounder control (adjustment, sensitivity analysis, dose–response) at most “moderate” (correlation ≠ causation). Items of the same design share the same bias, so only the strongest one counts, and the board warns when there is no experimental evidence at all. Theory/mechanism evidence and the mechanism in background knowledge are the same knowledge and count once. When the evidence barely moves the judgement, the board says the credence is what is left of the prior. When a universal (every) conclusion has counter-evidence, it asks whether that is a counterexample. When an inductive conclusion is used as a premise of a deductive set, the board also computes a lower bound on the credence of that deductive conclusion.
Connector statementTick “connector” next to a set's name to mark a sentence that is not a claim made in the book or paper but was added to connect the graph (it may be a hidden premise). It shows with a dashed border, and “view without connectors” on the result screen takes these sentences out or puts them back so you can compare how many pieces the graph falls into. When they are left out, they also leave the argument checks and the contradiction search. The mark is kept when you save the file.
Syllogism suggestions“Syllogism suggestions” at the bottom of the result screen scans the single statements by spelling and structure only and proposes candidates. ① Similar wording (spelling differences, a modified phrase typed as one word, near-identical strings) — if they are the same concept, a button unifies them. ② Two scattered statements that chain into a syllogism: “can be bound” if the conclusion is already there, “clash” if its opposite is there, and “follows but is not written” if it is nowhere. For “A is a B” and “A is a C”, the missing premise “every B is a C” is shown as a hidden-premise candidate, and a connector statement that yields a conclusion already in the book is reported as acting as a hidden premise. A suggestion that reads a sentence about a single subject (“the paradigm …”) as a claim about the whole class is marked. The code cannot tell whether two phrases mean the same or whether the book made the inference, so build the argument set with the button and check it yourself.
ModifiersA concept with no name of its own is built as head noun + modifiers. “flower swaying in wind” = head “flower” + modifier “swaying” (concept: sways · object: wind · preposition: in). Write the concept as a property (“is dangerous”, “sways”) so it meets the same property used elsewhere. In the graph it is a grey dot (●), and grey lines to flower, sways and wind spell out its definition. A modified concept is included in its head noun and in each modifier (an intersection), so from “a knife is a dangerous tool” the board can take “a knife is dangerous” and “a knife is a tool”. Modifiers in a different order give the same concept. A one-word modifier without an object goes before the noun (“dangerous tool”); anything longer goes after it.

Quantifiers: every (universal) · some (particular) · individual (a single thing such as Socrates or the Earth — treated like a universal in traditional logic; set number to plural for “other people and machines”).

Articles, agreement and negation are added automatically. Comparison, space and time relations recognise their converse as the same relation (“B is smaller than A” = “A is bigger than B”).

Inductive evaluation

Credence is the probability that the conclusion is true; reasoning quality is how well the argument was made. They differ — a sloppy argument can still have a true conclusion. The pale band on the bar is the range that reflects the uncertainty in the ratings you entered.
